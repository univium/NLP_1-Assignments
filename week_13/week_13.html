1.2.1. The Definition of a Narrative Poem

In literature, a poem-styled story is formally known as a narrative poem. Unlike lyric poetry, which focuses on personal emotions or fleeting moments, narrative poetry prioritizes plot, character development, and setting. It utilizes poetic devices—such as rhythm, rhyme, and imagery—to propel a sequence of events. Think of it as a bridge between the precision of a sonnet and the structural journey of a short story.

—

## The Keeper of the Clock

The gears were teeth that chewed the years, Inside a tower made of stone. Old Silas lived among the spheres, A king upon a brassy throne. He oiled the joints of Father Time, With heavy hands and silver hair, And listened to the hourly chime, That shuddered through the stagnant air.

One Tuesday noon, the rhythm broke, A spring had snapped—a jagged sound. The village froze beneath the smoke, And silence settled on the ground. The baker’s breath was trapped in frost, The falling rain hung like a bead. The world was still, its pulse was lost, A heartbeat stopped in mid-stride greed.

With steady fingers, Silas drew, A golden wire from his vest. He threaded history anew, And put the friction to the test. A single spark, a heavy thrum, The pendulum began its sway. The frozen birds began to hum, As Silas gave them back their day.

He watched the crowds begin to move, Unaware of the pause they’d known. He settled back into the groove, Once more a king, once more alone.

1.2.2. Within the literary canon, narrative poems are often categorized by their scope, the stature of their characters, and the stylistic treatment of their figures.

While all narrative poems tell a story, the “figures” (characters) and how they are presented define the specific sub-genre. Here are the primary styles:

—

## 1. The Epic (The Heroic Figure) The Epic is the “heavyweight” of narrative poetry. It focuses on a larger-than-life hero whose actions determine the fate of a nation, race, or the entire human universe. These figures are often semi-divine or possess extraordinary strength and moral complexity.

1.2.3. Key Characteristics: Vast settings, supernatural intervention, and “In Medias Res” (starting in the middle of the action).

1.2.4. Examples: Achilles in The Iliad, Beowulf in Beowulf, or Odysseus in The Odyssey.

## 2. The Mock-Epic (The Trivial Figure) This style uses the lofty, formal language of an Epic to describe a minor or trivial figure involved in a petty situation. It creates humor by applying “heroic” importance to something insignificant.

1.2.5. Key Characteristics: High-flown language used for low-stakes drama; satirical tone.

1.2.6. Example: Alexander Pope’s The Rape of the Lock, which treats the theft of a lock of hair like a grand battle between gods.

## 3. The Literary Ballad (The Tragic/Folk Figure) The ballad often centers on ordinary or legendary figures caught in moments of intense drama, romance, or tragedy. Unlike the Epic, the focus is narrow, usually revolving around a single event or a moral lesson.

1.2.7. Key Characteristics: Quatrains (four-line stanzas), repetition (refrains), and a rhythmic, musical quality.

1.2.8. Example: The wedding guest and the sailor in Coleridge’s The Rime of the Ancient Mariner.

## 4. The Idyll (The Pastoral Figure) An Idyll focuses on idealized figures, often shepherds or rural folk, living in a simplified, peaceful world. When used narratively, it often depicts a “golden age” or a legendary past.

1.2.9. Key Characteristics: Focus on “natural” nobility and the relationship between the figure and their environment.

1.2.10. Example: Tennyson’s Idylls of the King, which reimagines the knights of the Round Table through a lens of chivalric ideals.

—

1.2.11. Three Visions of the Keeper of Time

### I. The Epic: Silas, Ward of the Chronos

(A fragment from “The Chronomachia,” Book III)

Sing, Muse, of the mountain of gears, the silent, brass throne, Where Silas the Ward, ancient of days, ruled the moments alone. Not with sword, but with key, he held the cosmos at bay, And guarded the bubble where mortal men slept and they lay. Till the shadow-fracture struck! The great axle groaned and did bend, And time, in mid-motion, shattered. The bubble met its end! A thousand screams of silence rang as the people were froze, But Silas the Ward uprose! Majestic, his stature now grows. He wrestled the celestial engine, the gears locked in might, And hammered a path through the chronal fracture, restoring the light.

*

### II. The Ballad: The Weaver and the Wire

(Based on a traditional broadside folk tune)

Come all ye, and listen, and I will tell thee, Of Silas, a weaver, a plain sight to see. He lived in a tower, with shadows for friends, And tended the moments that gravity spends.

(REFRAIN: Sing silence, sing time, sing the thread that did mend.)

The baker, he paused, on his way to the shop, And the world, in a breath, came to a sudden full stop. The loom, it fell silent, the shuttle, it flew, For the great mainspring broken, the moments grew few.

Silas, he took up a single bright wire, And a single small spark, from a single small fire. He threaded the fracture, so quiet and keen, Till the heart of the tower beat steady and clean.

### III. Silas in the Idyll: The Golden Respiration

(A pastoral reverie)

Soft through the sun-dappled arch, where the ancient stonework sighs, Old Silas, gentle shepherd, works, with peace inside his eyes. He trims the delicate wings of the great clock’s escapement flight, Like guiding a butterfly on the currents of the light. The landscape outside stretches, a soft and emerald stream, Where children and their kites dance through a tranquil dream. A flock of woolly sheep grazes, a river soft and clear, All synchronized in a pastoral rhythm, held perfect by the year. For Silas knows the art, the slow and golden respiration, The gentle pulse that makes the moments of our small salvation.
